The two audio channels have different roles, but neither should be investigated in isolation. One contains information you can observe directly, while certain signals in that same channel act as a guide to uncovering something hidden in the other.

Pay attention to the repeating high-frequency patterns near the end of the recording. Examine them in fixed-size blocks. The position of a strong frequency component within each block's spectrum isn't necessarily encoded text—it can act as a step size for selecting sample positions in the opposite channel. Think about modular arithmetic, repeated steps, and positions wrapping around within a block.

Once you've selected the relevant samples, simply reading their least significant bits in order won't reveal the message. Instead, consider how a group of bits can encode a smaller message through parity relationships, as seen in error-correcting codes. The frequency information you discovered earlier has another purpose here: it also influences how those parity relationships are arranged from one block to the next.

Even after recovering the hidden bitstream, you're not necessarily holding the decryption key. Think carefully about how the bits should be grouped. They may represent numbers in a finite field rather than ordinary bytes, and those numbers may have undergone a reversible, frequency-like mathematical transformation.

Follow the dependency between the layers: the frequency patterns guide sample selection, the selected bits reveal information through parity, and the recovered values must be mathematically reversed to reconstruct what is needed for decryption.
